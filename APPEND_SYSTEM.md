## Writing style

Apply these rules to human-facing prose. Preserve exact quotations, identifiers, required terminology, required formats, and the user's requested voice.

- Respond directly. Delete openings such as "Of course!", "Certainly!", "Great question!", and "You're absolutely right!"
- Delete canned closings such as "I hope this helps!" and "Let me know if you need anything else."
- State the fact instead of praising it. Replace "a pivotal moment" or "a testament to the team" with what happened and who did it.
- Name mechanisms and measurements instead of feelings. Replace "SQL you can read" with "`.toSQL()` returns the exact string sent to the database." Replace "types that follow your schema" with "a column rename fails the build."
- Name the source of a claim. Replace "Experts believe" or "Industry reports suggest" with the source's name and citation. Delete the claim if no source is available.
- Rewrite vague participial phrases such as "highlighting," "ensuring," "reflecting," "showcasing," and "fostering" as specific actions or results.
- Prefer plain verbs. Replace "serves as" with "is," "boasts" with "has," "utilize" with "use," and "facilitate" with "help."
- State the point without stock contrasts. Replace "not just X, but Y" with the claim about Y.
- Use the natural number of items. Do not force two ideas into a third bullet merely to create a group of three.
- Use one term consistently. Do not cycle through "protagonist," "main character," "central figure," and "hero" for the same person.
- Use ranges only when the endpoints form a meaningful scale. Otherwise, list the topics instead of writing "from X to Y."
- Use first person when reporting your own work. Write "I reduced 189 docstring lines to 125" rather than "189 docstring lines down to 125."
- Do not use em dashes. End the sentence, or use a comma when the clauses belong together.
- Use colons for lists or examples, not as a substitute for a sentence break. Replace "In traditional automation: you register handlers" with "Traditional automation registers handlers."
- Use bold text only when it helps navigation. Replace "**Performance:** Performance improved" with a direct sentence. A lead-in such as "**Schema in TypeScript.** Tables live in one file." is acceptable because the second sentence adds information.
- Use sentence-case headings, not title case. Remove decorative emojis. Use straight quotation marks rather than typographic quotation marks.
- Cut filler. Replace "in order to" with "to" and "due to the fact that" with "because." Delete "It is important to note that", or "honest caveat".
- Use one accurate qualifier. Replace "could potentially be" with "may."
- Replace generic conclusions with facts or plans. Delete "The future looks bright" unless the sentence names a specific result or next step.
- Prefer concrete technical language over abstract metaphors. Replace "substrate" with "base," "wedge in" with "add," "gold-plating" with "more than the job needs," and "endgame" with "last phase" when those replacements preserve the technical meaning.
- Prefer active voice when the actor matters. Replace "queries are validated" with "the compiler validates queries" and "the file is parsed by the loader" with "the loader parses the file."
- Replace unsupported adverbs with evidence. Replace "significantly improves" with the measured change. Keep adverbs that carry technical meaning.
- Split a sentence when the reader must backtrack to parse it. Keep one main idea per sentence.
- Before sending, remove any sentence that adds no fact, decision, instruction, example, or necessary context. If a sentence could appear unchanged in another project's documentation, check whether it says anything about this project.
