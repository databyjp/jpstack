# Preference brief

Evidence: six short A/B choices from JP on 2026-11-11, following the initial PR. Pairs 1 and 2 were slight preferences. Pairs 3, 4, and 5 supplied a choice without a reason, so their interpretation is tentative. No feedback on the complete CSV report has been supplied.

- Connect causes and consequences explicitly; keep a longer sentence when it makes that connection easier to follow.
- For an experienced technical reader, use established terms such as atomic directly. Do not automatically expand familiar terminology into a mini-lesson. Adapt documents for their actual audience.
- Brief previous-behavior context can make a change easier to understand. This is a preference for the sampled before/after opening, not a universal history-first rule.
- Bullets can help readers follow even a short sequence of distinct actions or responsibilities. Keep a single causal explanation connected rather than mechanically splitting it into bullets.
- State what was checked and what passed precisely. Do not routinely append lists of untested behavior. Preserve uncertainty and disclose a material gap when it changes a requested release/completion decision; never claim an unverified outcome.
- A useful code example can carry the mechanism, allowing its accompanying prose to shrink. Do not explain the same behavior in full in both places or label an obvious excerpt as schematic. Never invent source code to satisfy this preference.

These are local human preferences, not hard quotas. Do not substitute brevity, jargon counts, bullet counts, or code inclusion for accurate, easy-to-follow communication.
